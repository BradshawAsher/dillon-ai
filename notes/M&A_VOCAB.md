1. EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization)
EBITDA is the most important metric for valuing a company because it shows the operating profitability of a business, regardless of how it is financed or its tax structure.
Why it matters: It acts as a proxy for cash flow. When companies are bought, they are typically valued as a multiple of their EBITDA (e.g., a "5x multiple" means you pay $5M for a company with $1M EBITDA).
The "Diligence Agent" perspective: Your agent must ensure that EBITDA is not being artificially inflated by the seller through "add-backs" (one-time expenses they claim won't happen again).
2. Quality of Earnings (QofE)
This is not a single number, but a forensic analysis of the company’s reported earnings.
Why it matters: It determines if the earnings are "sustainable" or "recurring."
What to look for: If the company had a massive spike in revenue due to a one-time contract that will not be renewed, that revenue should be stripped out of the "Quality of Earnings" to find the true, sustainable baseline.
3. Net Working Capital (NWC)
NWC is the difference between current assets and current liabilities.
Why it matters: In an M&A deal, you typically acquire the company "on a cash-free, debt-free basis" with a "normalized level of working capital".
The risk: If the seller aggressively drains their working capital (by not paying suppliers or rushing to collect cash from customers) right before the deal closes, they are essentially taking money out of your pocket after the deal is done.
4. Revenue Concentration
This measures the percentage of revenue generated by the top 5, 10, or 20 customers.
Why it matters: If a company relies on one or two major clients for the majority of its income, the business is extremely risky.
The "Diligence Agent" threshold: As established in our manifest, your agent should flag any single customer accounting for $>20\%$ of total revenue as a high-risk factor.
5. Capex (Capital Expenditures)
These are the funds used to acquire, upgrade, and maintain physical assets (like property, buildings, or equipment).
Why it matters: If the company reports high EBITDA but spends almost nothing on Capex, they might be "starving the business"—meaning they aren't maintaining their machinery, and you will have to pay for a massive equipment overhaul immediately after you buy it.
The Agentic Check: Cross-referencing the Fixed Asset Register against Maintenance Costs in the P&L is essential to detect this.
6. Burn Rate (for early-stage or high-growth deals)
The rate at which a company spends its available cash before becoming profitable.
Why it matters: If the target company has a high burn rate and you are acquiring them, you need to know exactly how much "runway" they have left before they run out of cash.
